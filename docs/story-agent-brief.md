## Intent

Develop a distinctive, emotionally compelling story for a touch-first restorative RPG about a returning Waykeeper.

The player initially appears to be repairing a damaged waterway, but gradually discovers that restoration is not equivalent to returning a system to its previous state. People, ecosystems, traditions and settlements have adapted to the years since the Stillfall. Restoring one relationship in the watershed can disrupt another.

The story should gain depth through:

- physical consequences
- character relationships
- disagreement
- changed routines
- environmental evidence
- architecture
- water movement
- letters and artifacts
- discoveries
- choices characters make independently of the player

Favor these over exposition, lore dumps, or NPCs explaining the world at length.

Restoration should remain fundamentally gentle and understandable while still involving genuine stakes.

Characters may disagree strongly without becoming villains, quest dispensers, or representatives of simplistic moral positions.

## Special Focus: Lio

The current Lio concept deserves particularly aggressive examination.

Do not begin from the assumption that:

> Lio closed the sluice, therefore the dramatic question is whether he was right or wrong.

Ask whether that is actually the most interesting version.

Explore alternatives such as:

- Lio correctly understood one danger but underestimated another.
- Lio's decision was sensible at the time but became harmful because conditions changed.
- Lio made a temporary intervention that later generations mistook for a permanent solution.
- Other people participated in the decision and Lio became the person history remembered.
- The physical infrastructure tells a more complicated story than the surviving accounts.
- Lio's decision protected something genuinely valuable while transferring risk elsewhere.
- Lio himself may have intended someone to reopen or reassess the system later.
- The letter may be truthful but incomplete rather than deceptive.
- Present-day characters may invoke Lio to support positions he never actually held.

These are prompts, not requirements. Develop the strongest interpretation you find.

Avoid:

- secret-villain Lio
- saintly misunderstood Lio
- a twist that simply proves one faction correct
- artificial moral ambiguity where consequences are actually obvious
- making the player judge a dead person's morality through a morality meter

The ideal result is that the player can understand why the sluice was closed, why reopening it is attractive, why leaving it closed has real costs, and why neither fact completely resolves what should happen now.

## Special Focus: The Wider Universe

Push particularly hard on the proposed connection between this world and Earth.

Do not assume that a surprising cosmological reveal automatically makes the story deeper.

Ask:

- Does an Earth connection deepen the emotional meaning of the waterway?
- Does it make the world feel larger or accidentally make it feel subordinate to Earth?
- Does it reinforce the game's themes or merely provide lore?
- Would players care more about Mara, Neri, Lio and the watershed afterward?
- Does the reveal recontextualize things the player has physically experienced?
- Could the same thematic effect be achieved without literal Earth?
- Would mystery be stronger than explanation?

The current story must remain completely meaningful if no literal Earth connection is ever revealed.

Do not use Earth as a shortcut for significance.

## Deliverables

### 1. Sharpen the Dramatic Spine

Define:

- the central dramatic question
- the protagonist's initial understanding of what a Waykeeper does
- the emotional misconception or limitation they begin with
- how that understanding changes
- the major turning point of Broken Ford
- the major turning point of Reedglass Marsh
- the Orchard turning point
- the climax of the first major arc
- the emotional resolution
- what remains deliberately unresolved afterward

The first major arc should have satisfying closure even if the larger universe story is never continued.

Avoid simply structuring the plot as:

> location is broken → player fixes location → next location

Each chapter should alter the player's understanding of restoration.

### 2. Build a Character Relationship Web

For every recurring character, specify:

- what they want
- what they fear
- what they believe about the watershed
- a contradiction in their personality or beliefs
- a meaningful past relationship with at least one other character
- something they do when the player is not directing them
- what would cause them to change their mind
- what they misunderstand
- how their daily behavior changes after particular restorations

Include at minimum:

- Mara
- Iona
- Oren
- Neri
- Daro
- Lio, through memory/evidence if appropriate

Do not make every relationship revolve around the protagonist.

Create relationships that visibly continue without the player.

Specify moments where the player can return somewhere and discover that two characters have acted, argued, reconciled, moved equipment, changed routines, altered irrigation, repaired something, written something, or made another decision since the player's last visit.

### 3. Build a Stillfall Continuity Timeline

Create a continuity matrix separating:

**A. Established facts** — Things existing content already makes canon.

**B. What each character believes happened** — These beliefs may conflict.

**C. What actually happened in the proposed version** — This is designer knowledge and does not necessarily need to become player knowledge.

**D. What can physically be discovered** — Examples: sediment, water marks, abandoned channels, altered masonry, repair scars, vegetation age, mechanisms, broken tools, letters, records, household adaptations, old footpaths, water-dependent architecture.

**E. When each character could reasonably learn each fact.**

Perform a continuity audit. Nobody should know information before they could realistically have obtained it. Prefer incomplete but defensible perspectives over everyone possessing convenient lore knowledge.

### 4. Design Playable Chapter Beats

Deepen the currently implemented chapters first: **Broken Ford** and **Reedglass Marsh**. Then propose **Counterweight Orchard** and the larger first-story arc.

For every major beat specify:

- **Before dialogue:** What does the player visually or physically notice first?
- **Player action:** What do they actually do?
- **Immediate change:** What happens while they are present?
- **Return-state change:** What is different if they return later?
- **Character consequence:** Who changes behavior because of it?
- **Ecological/social consequence:** What becomes better, worse, or simply different?
- **Unresolved question:** What remains uncertain afterward?

Whenever possible, a player should be able to understand the broad situation through observation before an NPC explains it.

### 5. Write Five Short Playable Scenes

Write phone-friendly scripts for:

1. Mara at the first basin
2. Iona after the spring
3. Oren before and after the landing receives water
4. Neri at her irrigation
5. the bell, Lio's letter, and Neri's response

Include:

- staging
- NPC movement
- environmental behavior
- player position where relevant
- concise dialogue
- pauses or reactions
- changes occurring during the conversation
- post-scene NPC behavior

Keep dialogue short enough to feel natural on a phone. Avoid speeches. Allow silence, gestures and environmental changes to carry meaning.

### 6. Design a Concrete Orchard Conflict

Counterweight Orchard should become the first major situation where “restore the water” is not an adequate answer.

Make Lio's protective choice understandable. Give downstream residents equally concrete and legitimate needs. The conflict must be visible in the environment rather than existing only in dialogue.

Develop at least two viable restorative approaches. Prefer three if all three are genuinely interesting.

For each approach define:

- what the player physically changes
- who benefits immediately
- who absorbs a cost
- ecological effects
- social effects
- visual world-state changes
- what characters think about the result
- what can be changed again later
- what cannot immediately be undone
- how the world adapts after the decision

Do not design:

- a morality meter
- good/bad alignment
- a secretly optimal solution
- a dialogue choice pretending to be systemic gameplay

Trade-offs should be recoverable where reasonable, allowing restoration itself to become iterative. A good solution today may need adjustment later.

### 7. Develop Three Universe Models

Explore three genuinely different models.

**Model A — Completely Self-Contained World**

No literal Earth connection is required. Explain what this adds, what this sacrifices, what mysteries remain, how it affects the Stillfall, and how it affects the emotional arc.

**Model B — Adjacent Worlds / Rare Confluences**

Connections exist but are unusual, partial or difficult. Explain what crosses between worlds, whether people can cross, whether water/ecology/memory/culture can cross, what evidence would fairly foreshadow this, and why it matters to ordinary people.

**Model C — Shared History / Far-Future Earth**

The world has some literal historical relationship to Earth. Explain what happened, how distant that history is, why contemporary characters do or do not know, what clues establish it fairly, what the reveal changes emotionally, and the danger of making the fantasy world feel like merely “post-apocalyptic Earth.”

For all three include:

- What it adds
- What it risks
- Required clues
- Character consequences
- Gameplay opportunities
- Whether the reveal is actually necessary

Then recommend one as **PROPOSED**, not canon.

Also state whether your strongest recommendation is to reveal it during the first arc, hint but delay, leave permanently ambiguous, or remove the connection entirely.

Do not choose an option merely because it creates the biggest lore twist.

### 8. Produce an Implementation Handoff

Map the proposal back onto the actual game.

For each scene or beat identify:

- existing location
- existing NPC
- existing world state
- existing mechanic involved
- new world state required
- new animation if required
- new prop/environment asset
- dialogue requirement
- persistence/save implication
- optional new mechanic
- dependencies

Avoid inventing expensive systems when an environmental change, NPC routine, animation, interactable object or small systemic extension can accomplish the same narrative goal.

Order implementation into small playable milestones. Each milestone should produce something that can be tested by an uncoached player.

For every milestone include acceptance checks such as:

- What did the player think happened?
- What did they notice before speaking to anyone?
- Did they understand what their action changed?
- Did they notice a consequence when returning?
- Could they explain why two characters disagreed?
- Did they feel they were restoring relationships rather than merely completing repair chores?
