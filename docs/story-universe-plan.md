# The Broken Waterway — story and universe plan

Status: **proposed narrative direction, 2026-09-27**. This is a planning document, not shipped content or an approved cosmology reveal. The [user-authored story-agent brief](story-agent-brief.md) explicitly asks the next designer to challenge and replace weak assumptions here. This plan builds on the playable [two-chapter story](story-chapters.md), the [RPG and restoration direction](rpg-systems-restoration-revision.md), and the [inhabited watershed prototype](open-world-direction.md). Existing scenes, saves, characters, Lio's letter, and the separate valley prototype remain intact.

## The dramatic promise

The Waykeeper comes home knowing how to mend a wound. They discover that the waterway is a network of living, engineered, and social relationships. A place can be physically repaired and still be wrong for those who live downstream. The player's growth is learning how to recognize what is broken, who bears the cost of a solution, and when restoring an old arrangement would repeat an old mistake.

**Core question:** When a system has changed around a wound, what does it mean to restore it well?

This is a source of drama, not a hidden morality test. Force, Anchor, Mend, Weave, Channel, containment, a temporary diversion, and even leaving an old channel closed can each be appropriate. Every major decision should make physical sense, involve people with understandable needs, and have a visible consequence. The game should remain satisfying for a player who simply enjoys the journey and restorations.

**Player promise:** “I can help this place live again, and I can see whose life changed because I did.”

## Story design rules

1. **Show a need before explaining it.** A dry landing, unused cups, absent letters, a bell nobody answers, or a person taking a long route should precede the quest text.
2. **Give every recurring person an agenda beyond the player.** They should be seen doing, waiting, choosing, disagreeing, and adjusting after restoration. Their schedules need only be authored at a few meaningful states; a complex simulation is unnecessary.
3. **Separate fact, memory, and interpretation.** Riverglass and Waykeeper techniques may reveal former physical patterns. They cannot prove a person's motive or decide what a community needs now. Testimony can be sincere and incomplete.
4. **Let the place carry the scene.** Dialogue pages stay short and touch friendly. Environment, gesture, music, silence, object motion, and NPC travel deliver much of the information.
5. **Do not surprise-punish care.** Before a consequential redirection, make the affected downstream need legible, allow inspection or conversation, preview the effect, and offer a recoverable way forward. The player should not have to reload a save to remain compassionate.
6. **Give restoration lasting consequences.** A changed river is followed by a changed route, occupation, resource, gathering, letter, or relationship. A thank-you line alone is insufficient.
7. **Preserve local autonomy.** The Waykeeper has unusual abilities; inhabitants have knowledge, authority, labor, and disagreements. The player helps them act rather than fixing their home while they stand idle.
8. **Keep mysteries answerable.** Ambiguity should lie in values and incomplete perspectives, not in whether the controls, world rules, or current quest are understandable.
9. **Protect the quiet.** Not every place needs a twist. Simple Mend moments, rest, humor, and ordinary life make the major choices matter.

## The world beneath the plot

The river-order civilization built ceramic channels, gauges, sluices, bridges and living reed works. Its “order” was a civic practice of tending relationships between water, land and settlements, rather than a perfect ancient empire. Its works were useful and beautiful, and some became brittle because they were designed around flows and communities that later changed.

Waykeeper techniques interact with understandable conditions: Force changes obstruction and movement; Anchor gives unstable structures or creatures footing; Mend repairs viable relationships; Weave connects compatible systems; Channel redirects Charge; Bloom helps already viable life flourish. None is a universal moral answer. An old riverglass imprint can show the shape a channel once held, while the present terrain, living organisms and people establish whether that shape is still healthy.

### Stillfall: proposed causal spine

Keep the storm real. Over years, upper waterworks were optimized for reliable delivery. Emergency diversions, sediment, deferred maintenance, changed habitats and competing withdrawals reduced the network's slack. The storm exposed those tensions. Some failures cascaded through channels; some gates were deliberately closed to protect people. Lio's closure saved the reservoir, while downstream places lost flow and contact. He is accountable for the costs of that choice and deserves to be heard before judged.

This is a **hypothesis for the writers**, not a fact to dump in dialogue or canonize before later chapters are authored. The eventual account should be consistent with observed water behavior and allow multiple perspectives: what a gauge records, what Lio remembers, what Neri lost, what Oren could and could not carry, and what older Waykeepers assumed. Avoid a late reveal that an evil mastermind caused every wound; that would flatten the playable restoration language.

### The protagonist

The protagonist returns with a river-mark, not a prophecy. Consider the mark a sign of training or a promise once made, rather than inherited proof that only this person can save the world. Mara knows the protagonist as a child of the ford; Iona remembers their early impatience or curiosity; Oren remembers a trip they once took together. Keep this past lightly authored so player appearance, Warden/Weaver affinity, equipment and skill choices remain theirs.

An emotional arc: **I know how to repair → I must ask who relies on this system → I can help people negotiate and sustain a living arrangement.** The player makes meaningful choices about methods, sequence, who to consult, and where to invest attention. The game should not pretend to offer a wholly branching epic it cannot build.

| **Iona** | To pass on a reliable tradition; she fears that Force will be mistaken for domination or that Anchor will preserve a bad structure. | She teaches through visible problems, then admits where the old manuals are incomplete. | **Oren** | To move people, letters and supplies again; he fears becoming the person who must choose whose message crosses first. | He moves from a dry landing to boat work; later routes and letters appear. | **Neri** | To know Lio is alive and keep the marsh fed; she fears his sacrifice will be dismissed or romanticized. | She resumes tending irrigated beds, then must decide what to say when his letter complicates the story. | Her bond with Lio includes affection and practical disagreement over water priorities. |
| **Daro** | To maintain community health and craft; he fears a restored spring whose new flow washes out the reed pocket. | Brewing and gathering change as water returns. He can offer a non-Waykeeper reading of the same environment. | His requests should sometimes differ from Neri's without making either person selfish. |
| **Lio** | To protect the reservoir and eventually account for downstream harm; he fears reopening too soon will collapse the upper system. | First encountered through consequences, traces, and a letter; later through his work and testimony. For each person, author a **before**, **during**, and **after** at one meaningful location. Use a small number of visible actions: carrying cups, repairing a boat, checking a gauge, tending beds, brewing, receiving a letter. A saved world-state flag should drive those actions so the place remembers the player's work.

## Story arc and release boundaries

The following is a planned spine. Times are playtest targets, not current runtime claims. Existing chapters remain playable while future content is authored incrementally.

| Part | Player question | Story and technique beat | Physical and social payoff | Status |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |

| **Ford expansion: A branch restored** | Who uses the water I release? | Learn Anchor, revisit an unstable place, listen to Oren, restore the upper ford weir with several techniques. | Water travels visibly to Oren's landing; his boat, workplace and route change. | Separate valley prototype, not integrated with chapter travel. |

| **Counterweight Orchard: The closed gate** | Was closing it wrong? | Meet Lio. Inspect reservoir pressure, dry downstream branch, and habitat now living in a former spillway. Learn Weave through a story-led act of reconnection. | A negotiated, testable flow arrangement; visible benefits and costs for more than one community. | Proposed, not implemented. |
| **Headwaters / shared watershed: A living agreement** | What should persist when the Waykeeper leaves? | Trace the cascade, combine techniques, revisit earlier sites with new knowledge, let residents establish maintenance and response practices. | A resilient network and changed routines; some scars remain, but people can care for them. | Proposed distant arc; no full new region until earlier milestones earn it. |

The ending should give emotional closure even if it seeds another story. Lio and Neri can disagree and remain family. The Waykeeper does not “finish nature.” A community gains the knowledge and agency to keep tending its world after the hero leaves.

## Four authored turning points

### 1. Mara's cups

Before the first Mend, the player sees unused cups and a dry basin. After the grove and spring recover, Mara carries them to a small gathering. She tells a short personal memory only when the player returns. The water change creates hospitality; the scene proves that restoration serves life, not a progress meter.

### 2. Oren's landing

The player hears Oren's need and can pin the weir, while its dry channel and boat make the issue visible. Force clears the intake, Anchor steadies it, Mend lets water run. The camera follows the first water into the landing; Oren walks to the basin and later uses his boat. The current V2a prototype provides the state change; the next narrative pass should give it a short, human follow-through rather than adding more instructions.

### 3. The bell and the letter

The bell returns because the garden, nursery and channel have become viable, not because the player activated a story switch. Oren recognizes a pattern; Neri anticipates news and fears it. Lio's letter answers whether he lives while raising the harder question of his choice. Let the player see Neri respond physically before asking them to read another page of text.

### 4. The orchard's two banks

Show the reservoir serving one community, a dry branch serving another, and an organism or settlement that adapted to the closure. Lio demonstrates why he closed the gate. Opening it at once would create a legible problem. The player investigates an alternative: stabilize a weak bank, reconnect a secondary course, or stage water through a temporary route. They preview and test a flow change, then hear both communities react. No solution is framed as a moral score. The result is recorded in the world and can be revised through another authored interaction.

**Possible physical layout, for preproduction:** an old main outlet leads toward Reedglass. Its fractured channel bed would fail under a full release. Lio's closed sluice routes pressure into orchard relief terraces; a new wetland now relies on that water. A narrow side course can carry a partial test flow. The player first sees the dry downstream need and the functioning new habitat, then learns why a full opening is hazardous. Two viable authored strategies could be: reinforce the old outlet with Warden skills and preserve enough terrace water through a small bypass; or use Weave to share and balance a limited flow while a slower living support system takes root. Both require discussion with residents and a visible trial. Both leave a maintenance commitment; neither grants a perfect, cost-free “best” ending. These are candidates, not implemented puzzle requirements.

## Choice without a fake morality system

Track **facts and commitments**, not good/evil points. Suggested compact ledger:

- `discovered`: who or what the player has actually encountered;
- `heard`: testimony received from Mara, Iona, Oren, Neri, Daro, Lio;
- `condition`: each ecological/structural state already used by restoration;
- `commitment`: a small number of promises or community decisions;
- `outcome`: physical flow and social consequences, derived from stable choices;
- `witnessed`: whether the player has seen a changed routine or scene.

Do not give XP for repeatedly asking the same question. Do not hide essential technique access behind a dialogue opinion. Optional dialogue may change trust, context, a line at the reunion, or who helps with a later restoration. The major water decision should offer more than one viable technique route while keeping the story manageable: distinct method and scene variations can converge on a stable shared outcome.

Every major quest needs: **visible wound → human stake → investigative clue → restorative action → immediate physical result → delayed social result → journal memory/return reason**. At least one late return should reveal that earlier restoration created a new possibility, not simply another task marker.

## Could this connect to our universe?

There are two valuable meanings here. **Thematic connection** means the fiction helps players notice water, infrastructure, care and interdependence in their own lives. **Literal connection** means Earth and the Waykeeper world share a physical or metaphysical history. The first can be present now. The second should be chosen deliberately and earned over time.

| Model | Story possibilities | Main risk |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Self-contained world** | Full freedom to develop river-order culture and natural laws. Familiar patterns can resonate with Earth without naming it. | The real-world connection remains emotional rather than a lore reveal. |
| **Adjacent worlds with rare confluences — recommended exploration** | The worlds are complete places. Under exceptional conditions, water or riverglass can carry limited patterns across a boundary. A late discovery could widen the story without changing the genre of the opening. | A premature portal/reveal would make existing communities feel like setup for Earth lore. |
| **Earth's distant future or hidden geography** | Strong archaeological surprise; familiar artifacts gain new meaning. | It demands rigorous history, climate, chronology and cultural explanation, and can shrink the world's originality. |
| **A story or simulation inside our world** | Meta narrative about what we choose to tend. | It can cheapen the inhabitants' reality and the player's restoration work. |

**Recommendation:** Write the first complete arc so it works as an autonomous world. Internally reserve the adjacent-world model as a *possible* later truth. The player should not need Earth to care about Mara, Neri or Lio. Avoid an isekai protagonist, modern brand artifacts, a secret Earth villain, or a final scene that declares the riverworld “only” a simulation.

### If adjacent worlds become canon: constraints

1. A confluence transmits **limited traces or patterns**, not unlimited people, power or messages. It cannot become a shortcut that solves the reservoir conflict.
2. Its phenomena obey observable rules: rare conditions, locality, energy/material cost, and imperfect interpretation. Define these before writing a reveal.
3. Earth is neither the riverworld's source nor its judge. Both are real and have agency; neither exists as a metaphor for the other.
4. The protagonist has no inherited Earth destiny. The Waykeeper's competence comes from practice and relationships.
5. No Earth answer overrides local testimony. A riverglass image of an unfamiliar crossing can be intriguing without proving who built it or why.
6. Accessibility: any cosmic mystery must be optional to completing a restorative arc, and its clues must be perceivable without audio alone.

### Reveal ladder and exit points

- **First arc, optional echo:** an unfamiliar pattern appears in riverglass or a weathered gauge. It has an ordinary in-world explanation available. No character announces Earth.
- **Later investigation:** independent clues suggest a second watershed or another kind of sky. Characters disagree over what the evidence means. Each clue is a world observation, not a long lore entry.
- **Only after a complete local story:** a verifiable confluence event could establish contact. The player still has a local reason to care about what happens next.
- **Exit option:** if testing finds the connection distracting, stop at the first echo. The world and first arc remain coherent.

Before canonizing a literal link, answer: What can cross? Who knew? Why has it not transformed daily life? What does it cost? What can both worlds learn from each other? What story could only this connection tell? If the answers are weak, preserve thematic connection and keep the mystery open.

**Candidate clue, if the adjacent-world model earns a place:** at a late headwater site, riverglass briefly holds the reflection of a straight-edged rain crossing unlike river-order stonework. The player can inspect its material pattern, but the image carries no label, date, person or instruction. An inhabitant reasonably thinks it is a lost local city. Only a second independent observation elsewhere could make a different-world hypothesis credible. This should never be presented as proof that the setting is Earth, or as a collectible required for the first story's ending.

## Presentation and pacing

- **Dialogue:** 1–3 short touch pages per beat, one concrete emotion/action per page. Optional questions add texture. Do not put technique instructions and a major emotional confession into the same paragraph.
- **Character staging:** approach, work animation, eye line, small object handling and change of position before a portrait panel. Use NPC portraits to support a moment, not carry it alone.
- **Restoration scenes:** a clear physical chain from cause to consequence. Follow water or roots far enough for the player to understand downstream effects; avoid long unskippable cinematics on repeat or reload.
- **Audio:** compose future bell, flow and settlement motifs, with subtitles, visible motion and haptics as alternatives. Keep the project Master muted during prototyping until the user asks for sound again.
- **Journal:** distinguish observed fact, a person's account, and the player's chosen pin. Do not display undiscovered site solutions. Record a social consequence after it is actually seen.
- **Mobile:** stage close, high-contrast gestures and meaningful silhouettes; no lore clue should depend on reading tiny environmental text or making a precise touch selection.
- **Performance:** authored routine changes should be event/state based, not dozens of always-running NPC simulations. Measure on phone before expanding scene density.

## Implementation plan and acceptance

### Phase N0 — story bible and evidence map

**This document is the draft.** Write a one-page timeline of what each character knew before, during and after Stillfall; a watershed map showing water dependence; and a clue ledger separating known facts, hypotheses and future reveals. Review with the design agent. Decide whether the real-world link stays thematic or remains a reserved adjacent-world possibility. Do not canonize an Earth connection by accident in prop art or dialogue.

### Phase N1 — deepen the existing opening

Keep the playable sequence. Add a few authored before/after acts for Mara and Iona, tighten their dialogue, stage the first return gathering, and record player-observed changes in the journal. Add one optional conversation that reveals the protagonist's prior relationship without fixing their personality. **Acceptance:** an uncoached player can say who Mara and Iona are, why the spring needs Force, and what the first restoration changed for people.

### Phase N2 — make the valley inhabited

Give Oren one brief physical action at the dry landing and one after water returns. Connect the local weir story to the broader Ford without confusing it with Lio's reservoir sluice. Add one more social consequence on a separate trail, such as a restored crossing enabling a courier or Daro's resource arrival. **Acceptance:** in a 10–15 minute slice, the player sees two people's routines change without being told that the world is “alive.” Preserve separate prototype saves until connected-region migration is planned.

### Phase N3 — strengthen Reedglass without adding a region

Stage Neri's worry and work before and after irrigation; give Daro a practical concern that complicates “more water is always better”; make Oren's recovered letter an event seen in space. Revise lines to distinguish what each person knows about Lio. **Acceptance:** the player understands Lio's stake before reading his letter and can return to witness Neri's changed life. Story flags and rewards remain duplicate-proof.

### Phase N4 — preproduce Orchard before building it

Make a watershed dependency map, character testimony timeline, Orchard site states, three or fewer core decisions, a Weave teaching moment, and an honest scene showing the trade-off of opening the gate. Prototype one choice with reversible preview and two viable technique approaches. **Gate:** a paper walkthrough explains the conflict without a villain, a magic answer, precision inputs, or an arbitrary switch puzzle. Only then budget full third-region art and implementation.

### Phase N5 — evaluate the universe connection

Playtest an arc with no literal Earth clue and a variant with one optional echo. Ask what the player inferred, whether it distracted from the water conflict, and whether it made the world feel more or less real. Choose a canon only when it increases the human story and gives later gameplay a distinct purpose. A planned reveal is not a reason to postpone a satisfying ending to the local arc.

## Narrative QA questions

- Can a player describe a character's desire without naming their quest reward?
- Can they explain a restoration's downstream effect from what they saw, before reading the journal?
- Does every major character do at least one consequential thing the player did not command?
- Are Lio's protective reason and downstream cost both legible, without the game declaring an answer for the player?
- Do major choices have previews, understandable consequences and a path to repair unintended harm?
- Can a player finish and value the first arc without learning whether Earth exists in this cosmology?
- If the Earth connection is removed, do the characters, culture and restoration mechanics still feel original and complete?
